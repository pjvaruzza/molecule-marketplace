# Hydrogen Marketplace — Conversation-Readiness Reading List

**Purpose:** Get fluent enough that a chlor-alkali plant manager, a heat-treat shop owner, and a hazmat carrier each conclude you understand their world. Organized by the conversation each item prepares you for, not by topic. Free unless marked [$].

The honest framing: "sounding dumb" in industrial gas is almost never about missing deep theory — it's about units, vocabulary, and not knowing the other side's alternatives. Part 1 fixes 70% of the risk in an afternoon. Parts 2–6 build the depth that turns calls into LOIs.

---

## Part 1 — The fluency cheat sheet (read first, internalize cold)

### Units — the #1 tell

The gas industry talks in **standard cubic feet (SCF)**, not kilograms. Conversions to know without thinking:

- **1 kg H2 ≈ 423 SCF ≈ 11.13 Nm³** (1 lb ≈ 192 SCF)
- A steel jumbo-tube trailer listed at ~163,000 SCF is ~385 kg — matches the "380 kg steel trailer" figure. Sanity-check any trailer spec sheet this way.
- Producers quote output in **tons/day or SCFM**; buyers think in **SCFH** flow to the furnace. A heat treater "running 2,000 SCFH" for two shifts ≈ 32,000 SCF/day ≈ **75 kg/day** — do this arithmetic live on calls and you instantly separate yourself from every other cold-caller.
- Pressure: trailers at 2,400–3,600 psi legacy (165–250 bar); newer composite 350–540 bar. DOT limits matter here.

### Vocabulary that signals insider vs. tourist

- **Tube trailer / jumbo tubes**, never "tanker" (tankers carry liquid). Liquid H2 is a different product, different trucks, different customers.
- **Swap** (full-for-empty exchange), **fill plant/fill point**, **telemetry** (remote tank level monitoring), **demurrage/trailer rent**, **COA** (certificate of analysis), **deoxo + dryer** (catalytic O2 removal + desiccant drying — what chlor-alkali H2 needs to hit spec), **take-or-pay**, **facility fee**, **hazmat surcharge**.
- **UN1049** = compressed hydrogen (your product). UN1966 = refrigerated liquid (not your product).
- Purity nomenclature: **Grade 4.5 = 99.995%, 5.0 = 99.999%** ("four-five", "five-oh"). The governing spec is **CGA G-5.3, Commodity Specification for Hydrogen** — know it exists and that COAs reference it. Heat treaters typically need ≥99.95% with a **low dew point** (bright annealing wants very dry gas — moisture, not ppm impurities, is often their real spec).
- Chlor-alkali language: **ECU** (electrochemical unit — the bundled economics of ~1 ton chlorine + 1.1 ton caustic + ~28 kg H2), **membrane cell** (the dominant modern process), **cell room**.

### Killer facts to deploy (all sourced in Parts 2–5)

- Chlor-alkali co-produces ~28 kg H2 per ton of caustic; in Europe **10–15% of byproduct H2 goes unused** — equivalent to a 200 MW electrolyzer's entire output. Case studies show single plants venting 40%+ of their hydrogen. With a producer: "What's your current utilization — boiler fuel, HCl synthesis, vented?" is the question that shows you know their three default sinks.
- Production is **<20% of delivered hydrogen cost** — packaging and logistics dominate. This is your whole thesis in one stat, and it's why a producer's "we sell at the fence for $2" and a buyer's "we pay $11 delivered" can both be true.
- Composite trailers carry **2–3× steel payload** (560–900 vs ~380 kg) — the single biggest lever on per-kg freight.

### Faux pas to avoid

- Don't pitch green hydrogen economics to grey-market buyers; heat treaters buy molecules, not credits. (Know the 45V/colors vocabulary; don't lead with it.)
- Don't propose volumes a trailer can't serve — you now know a 30 kg/day account parks a trailer for 3 weeks. Suggesting it flags you as someone who's never seen the asset math.
- Know the buyer's BATNAs before claiming to beat them: **dissociated ammonia** (75% H2/25% N2 — the legacy cheap atmosphere), **N2–H2 blends**, **on-site generation** (small PEM electrolyzers, SMR-in-a-box, ammonia crackers). If a buyer says "we run DA," you should know exactly what that means and why some shops are switching away (ammonia handling, consistency) — that's the wedge.
- With producers, never imply their H2 is "waste." It's byproduct with an opportunity cost; they have a boiler that burns it at natural-gas value. Your offer competes with ~$0.30–0.60/kg fuel value, and saying so out loud earns respect.

## Part 2 — Before producer calls (chlor-alkali supply side)

1. **Euro Chlor, "Hydrogen from chlor-alkali production" info sheet + FOCS-13 leaflet** — eurochlor.org/publication/hydrogen-from-chlor-alkali-production/ and eurochlor.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/FOCS-13-H2.pdf. Free, short, and the source of the utilization stats above. European data, but the process economics translate; US producers know these documents.
2. **Khasawneh et al., "Utilization of hydrogen as clean energy resource in chlor-alkali process" (Energy Exploration & Exploitation, 2019, open access)** — the plant-level case study: 47% to HCl synthesis, 43% vented. Teaches you the internal-sinks framework producers actually use.
3. **MDPI Hydrogen (2025), "Life Cycle GHG Analysis of the Chlor-Alkali Process and By-Product Hydrogen in the United States"** — US-specific; also flags compression energy as the gap between cell-room H2 and merchantable H2 — exactly the fill-point question in your diligence list.
4. **Olin investor presentations + Hidrogenii (Olin/Plug JV) press releases** — free on Olin IR. How the most sophisticated US chlor-alkali player frames H2 monetization; their language becomes your language.
5. **Chlorine Institute (US) public materials** — the US counterpart to Euro Chlor; skim their hydrogen-relevant pamphlet list so you can reference it.
6. Skim one **membrane-cell process explainer** (any reputable source — Euro Chlor's own process pages work) so "anolyte/catholyte/cell room" don't rattle you. 30 minutes, no more.

## Part 3 — Before buyer calls (heat treating first, then food/glass)

1. **Heat Treat Today (heattreattoday.com) + Dan Herring's "Heat Treat Doctor" columns** — free, current, and written in the exact register your buyers speak. Read everything tagged atmospheres/hydrogen; start with the hydrogen-embrittlement explainers so you can field the inevitable safety question.
2. **[$] Herring, *Atmosphere Heat Treatment*, Vol. II (BNP Media, 2015)** — the definitive furnace-atmospheres reference: N2–H2 blends, dew point control, bright annealing, DA. Vol. I (2014) if you want the metallurgy fundamentals. This is the single highest-ROI paid purchase on this list — one book makes you conversant with every annealing shop in Texas.
3. **Industrial Heating + Thermal Processing magazines** — free trade pubs; scan atmosphere-related archives. Their advertisers double as a map of who sells gas equipment to your buyers.
4. **Metal Treating Institute (MTI)** — the commercial heat treaters' association; member directory ≈ your Gulf Coast prospect list, and their events are buyer-side networking your playbook's CGA strategy doesn't cover.
5. **Airgas/Linde/Air Products hydrogen product pages** — read them as competitive intelligence: the cylinder → MicroBulk → bulk → on-site ladder is the incumbent offer you're wedging into.
6. Food-grade (lighter touch): know that hydrogenation buyers need food-grade documentation and FDA-compliant COAs; one afternoon on FDA food-grade gas requirements suffices until you have a live prospect.
7. On-site generation BATNAs: skim **Nel, Plug, HyGear** product pages for small on-site units — know the capex class and footprint a buyer's alternative implies.

## Part 4 — Logistics, safety, and regulatory fluency

1. **CGA standards — know these exist and what each covers** (you get library access with the CGA membership already in your playbook): **G-5** (hydrogen fundamentals), **G-5.3** (commodity spec/purity grades — read this one fully), **G-5.4** (piping at user sites), **G-5.5** (vent systems), **H-5** (bulk supply systems — referenced by NFPA 55). Free hack until membership clears: **AIGA publishes harmonized free PDFs of several CGA standards** (e.g., AIGA 087 = CGA G-5.4) at asiaiga.org.
2. **NFPA 2 (Hydrogen Technologies Code) + NFPA 55** — awareness level only; these are what a buyer's safety officer and fire marshal will cite when siting a trailer pad. Knowing setback distances exist (and who governs them) is enough.
3. **h2tools.org (PNNL Hydrogen Tools)** — free: properties calculators, unit converters, best practices, and a lessons-learned incident database. Read five incident reports; nothing builds safety credibility faster than referencing real failure modes unprompted.
4. **DOE Hydrogen Delivery pages + HDSAM (Argonne's delivery cost model)** — the vocabulary of delivery economics; HDSAM is the model behind every $/kg delivery figure in the literature, including ours.
5. **FMCSA hazmat + PHMSA offeror materials** — already in your playbook's compliance section; re-read after Parts 2–3 and the offeror/broker distinction will land differently.

## Part 5 — Market data and news flow (additions to your existing list)

Your playbook already has Gasworld, S&P Global Commodity Insights, Global Hydrogen Review, Hydrogen Central, EIA. Add:

1. **Hydrogen Insight (hydrogeninsight.com)** — best free-ish daily news desk on hydrogen commercial deals; their offtake-agreement coverage is your deal-structure education.
2. **EIA Today in Energy hydrogen pieces + AEO Hydrogen Market Module assumptions** — free, and quoting EIA numbers is the cheapest credibility there is.
3. **Olin, Westlake, Linde, Air Products quarterly calls (transcripts free)** — listen for how majors talk about merchant hydrogen, packaged gas pricing, and small-customer economics. One earnings season of this and you'll hear the gap you're filling described by the incumbents themselves.

## Part 6 — The trading and marketplace craft layer

1. **Pirrong, *The Economics of Commodity Trading Firms*** (free PDF, Trafigura-commissioned) — the intellectual frame for what you actually are: a physical-commodity intermediary earning a spread for transformation in space, time, and form. Chapter on risk (basis, credit, operational — not flat price) maps one-to-one onto your back-to-back model. The best 90 pages you'll read for this business.
2. **[$] Blas & Farchy, *The World for Sale*** — the culture and instincts of physical trading; teaches you how counterparties think about optionality, exclusivity, and information.
3. **Bill Gurley, "A Rake Too Far"** (free, abovethecrowd.com) — the canonical essay on take-rate strategy; directly relevant to the $0.50-vs-$2.50/kg decision in your unit economics v2.
4. **[$] Andrew Chen, *The Cold Start Problem*** — systematizes the supply-first/atomic-network logic your playbook already follows; useful mostly for investor-conversation vocabulary.
5. **Ryan Petersen long-form interviews** (Invest Like the Best has several) — Flexport's manual-first years, cold outreach as GTM, and complexity-as-moat, from the founder your playbook already cites.
6. **[$] Almqvist, *History of the Industrial Gases*** (Springer) — optional depth: why the majors are structured the way they are, why the industry consolidated, why relationships dominate. Explains the century-old incumbency you're routing around.

## Part 7 — Two-week crash sequence

**Days 1–2:** Part 1 cheat sheet until the unit conversions are reflexive. Open h2tools.org converters and drill.
**Days 3–4:** Producer side — Euro Chlor docs, Khasawneh paper, Olin IR deck. Draft your five producer discovery questions (utilization split, fill-point pressure, trailer ownership, purity/COA capability, who handles their merchant sales today).
**Days 5–7:** Buyer side — Heat Treat Today hydrogen archive, order Herring Vol. II, MTI directory scan. Draft buyer discovery questions (current supplier, delivered price, contract minimums, flow rate in SCFH, atmosphere type — pure H2 vs blend vs DA).
**Days 8–9:** CGA G-5.3 cover to cover + five h2tools incident reports + AIGA 087 skim.
**Days 10–11:** Pirrong + Gurley's rake essay. Revisit your pricing posture with both in your head.
**Days 12–14:** One earnings transcript each from Olin and a gas major; Hydrogen Insight backlog; then do a full mock producer call and mock buyer call out loud — recorded — and listen for unit slips.

After this sequence you'll know more about the *intersection* of these three worlds — byproduct supply, small-buyer atmospheres, hazmat logistics — than almost anyone you call. That intersection is the company.
